Biology Chapter 3: Enzymes

Enzymes are biological catalysts that speed up chemical reactions in living organisms without being permanently consumed in the reaction.

MDCAT Important: Enzymes lower the activation energy required for a chemical reaction.

1. What Are Enzymes?

An enzyme is a biological catalyst that increases the rate of a biochemical reaction without undergoing permanent chemical change.

  • Most enzymes are proteins.
  • Some RNA molecules can also act as catalysts; these are called ribozymes.
  • Enzymes are highly specific.
  • Enzymes are not permanently consumed during the reaction.
  • Enzyme activity is affected by temperature and pH.

2. Activation Energy

Activation energy is the minimum energy required for reactants to reach the transition state and begin a chemical reaction.

Enzymes speed up reactions by lowering the activation energy.

Remember: Enzymes lower activation energy, but they do not change the overall energy released or absorbed by the reaction.

3. Enzyme Structure

The three-dimensional shape of an enzyme is very important because it determines how the enzyme interacts with its substrate.

Active Site

The active site is the specific region of an enzyme where the substrate binds and the reaction takes place.

  • It has a specific three-dimensional shape.
  • The substrate binds to the active site.
  • Catalysis occurs at or near the active site.

4. Substrate

A substrate is the reactant molecule upon which an enzyme acts.

The enzyme binds to its substrate to form an enzyme-substrate complex.

Basic sequence:

Enzyme + Substrate → Enzyme-Substrate Complex → Enzyme + Products

5. Lock-and-Key Model

According to the lock-and-key model, the active site of an enzyme has a shape complementary to its substrate.

The substrate fits into the active site in a way similar to a key fitting into a lock.

MDCAT Point: The lock-and-key model helps explain enzyme specificity.

6. Induced-Fit Model

The induced-fit model states that the active site can undergo a slight change in shape when the substrate binds.

This helps the enzyme interact more effectively with the substrate and promote the reaction.

Exam Tip: Lock-and-key = pre-existing complementary shape.
Induced fit = active site adjusts when substrate binds.

7. Enzyme Specificity

Enzymes are generally specific because their active sites interact with particular substrates.

The shape, chemical properties and arrangement of groups in the active site contribute to this specificity.

8. Factors Affecting Enzyme Activity

A. Temperature

Increasing temperature generally increases the rate of enzyme activity up to an optimum temperature.

Above the optimum temperature, many enzymes lose their normal three-dimensional structure and activity decreases.

Denaturation: Excessive heat can disrupt the structure of an enzyme and cause loss of its activity.

B. pH

Each enzyme has a pH range in which it works most effectively. Extreme pH can alter the structure of an enzyme and reduce its activity.

C. Substrate Concentration

When substrate concentration increases, enzyme activity generally increases until the available active sites become occupied.

After saturation, adding more substrate does not produce a proportional increase in reaction rate.

9. Enzyme Saturation

At high substrate concentration, most or all available active sites may be occupied by substrate molecules.

At this point, the enzyme is said to be saturated and the reaction approaches its maximum rate under those conditions.

10. Enzyme Inhibitors

An inhibitor is a substance that decreases the activity of an enzyme.

Competitive Inhibition

A competitive inhibitor competes with the substrate for the active site.

  • It resembles the substrate in some way.
  • It binds to the active site.
  • It prevents or reduces substrate binding.
  • Increasing substrate concentration can reduce its effect in many cases.

Non-Competitive Inhibition

A non-competitive inhibitor binds at a site other than the active site and can alter the enzyme's shape and activity.

Remember: Competitive inhibitor → competes for active site.
Non-competitive inhibitor → binds at another site and affects enzyme activity.

11. Cofactors

Some enzymes require non-protein components called cofactors for proper activity.

Cofactors may include inorganic ions such as metal ions.

Coenzymes

Coenzymes are organic molecules that assist enzymes in carrying out biochemical reactions. Many coenzymes are derived from vitamins.

12. Apoenzyme and Holoenzyme

Apoenzyme

The protein part of an enzyme that requires a non-protein component is called the apoenzyme.

Holoenzyme

The complete, active enzyme consisting of the apoenzyme together with its required cofactor is called a holoenzyme.

Simple formula:

Apoenzyme + Cofactor = Holoenzyme

13. Denaturation of Enzymes

Denaturation is the disruption of the normal three-dimensional structure of a protein.

Because enzyme activity depends on its structure, denaturation can cause loss of enzyme activity.

  • High temperature can cause denaturation.
  • Extreme pH can cause denaturation.
  • The active site may lose its proper shape.

14. Intracellular and Extracellular Enzymes

Intracellular Enzymes

These enzymes function inside cells.

Extracellular Enzymes

These enzymes are secreted outside the cells where they perform their functions.

Digestive enzymes are common examples of extracellular enzymes.

15. Important Enzymes

Enzyme Major Function
Amylase Breaks down starch
Pepsin Digests proteins in the stomach
Trypsin Digests proteins in the small intestine
Lipase Breaks down lipids
Sucrase Breaks down sucrose
Lactase Breaks down lactose

17. MDCAT Practice MCQs

1. Enzymes are biological:

A) Hormones

B) Catalysts

C) Vitamins

D) Lipids

Answer: B) Catalysts

2. Enzymes mainly speed up reactions by:

A) Increasing activation energy

B) Decreasing activation energy

C) Increasing product energy

D) Changing the final products

Answer: B) Decreasing activation energy

3. The region where a substrate binds to an enzyme is called:

A) Nucleus

B) Active site

C) Matrix

D) Cell wall

Answer: B) Active site

4. A substance upon which an enzyme acts is called:

A) Cofactor

B) Product

C) Substrate

D) Inhibitor

Answer: C) Substrate

5. Which model describes the active site as adjusting its shape when the substrate binds?

A) Lock-and-key model

B) Induced-fit model

C) Cell theory

D) Fluid mosaic model

Answer: B) Induced-fit model

6. High temperature can reduce enzyme activity because it may cause:

A) Replication

B) Denaturation

C) Photosynthesis

D) Diffusion

Answer: B) Denaturation

7. A competitive inhibitor generally binds to the:

A) Active site

B) Nucleus

C) Cell wall

D) Ribosome

Answer: A) Active site

8. Which enzyme digests starch?

A) Lipase

B) Amylase

C) Pepsin

D) Lactase

Answer: B) Amylase

9. The protein part of a complex enzyme is called:

A) Coenzyme

B) Apoenzyme

C) Substrate

D) Product

Answer: B) Apoenzyme

10. Apoenzyme plus its required cofactor forms a:

A) Substrate

B) Holoenzyme

C) Product

D) Inhibitor

Answer: B) Holoenzyme
