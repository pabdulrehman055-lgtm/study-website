📚 What Are Enzymes?

Enzymes are biological catalysts that increase the rate of biochemical reactions without being permanently consumed during the reaction.

MDCAT Important: Enzymes lower the activation energy required for a reaction.

1. Enzymes as Biological Catalysts

A catalyst is a substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without being permanently consumed. Enzymes act as biological catalysts inside living organisms.

  • Most enzymes are proteins.
  • Some RNA molecules can also act as catalysts; these are called ribozymes.
  • Enzymes are generally highly specific.
  • Enzymes are not permanently consumed during reactions.
  • Enzyme activity depends on suitable conditions.

2. Activation Energy

Activation energy is the minimum energy required for reactants to reach the transition state and start a chemical reaction.

Enzymes make biochemical reactions easier by lowering the activation energy.

Enzyme → Lowers Activation Energy → Faster Reaction
Remember: An enzyme lowers activation energy but does not change the overall energy change of the reaction.

3. Enzyme Structure

The three-dimensional structure of an enzyme is important for its function. The shape and chemical properties of its active site allow it to interact with particular substrates.

Active Site

The active site is the specific region of an enzyme where the substrate binds and where catalysis occurs.

  • It has a specific three-dimensional arrangement.
  • The substrate binds to the active site.
  • The reaction occurs at or near the active site.
MDCAT Point: The active site is responsible for much of an enzyme's specificity.

4. Substrate and Enzyme-Substrate Complex

A substrate is the reactant molecule upon which an enzyme acts.

When a substrate binds to an enzyme, an enzyme-substrate complex is formed.

Enzyme + Substrate → Enzyme-Substrate Complex → Enzyme + Products

5. Lock-and-Key Model

The lock-and-key model explains enzyme specificity by suggesting that the active site has a shape complementary to the substrate.

The substrate fits into the active site in a way similar to a key fitting into a lock.

MDCAT Point: Lock-and-key model → specific complementary shape.

6. Induced-Fit Model

According to the induced-fit model, the active site can undergo a slight conformational change when the substrate binds.

This allows the enzyme and substrate to interact effectively and promotes catalysis.

Model Main Idea
Lock-and-Key Active site is considered complementary to the substrate.
Induced-Fit Active site adjusts its shape when substrate binds.

7. Enzyme Specificity

Enzymes are generally specific because their active sites interact with particular substrates.

The shape and chemical properties of the active site determine which substrate can interact effectively with the enzyme.

Exam Tip: Remember: enzyme specificity is closely related to the structure and properties of the active site.

8. Effect of Temperature on Enzyme Activity

Increasing temperature generally increases enzyme activity up to an optimum temperature because molecules move faster and collisions become more frequent.

Above the optimum temperature, many enzymes lose their normal three-dimensional structure and their activity decreases.

Denaturation: Excessive heat can disrupt the structure of an enzyme and cause a loss of activity.

9. Effect of pH on Enzyme Activity

Every enzyme has a pH range in which it works most effectively. This is called its optimum pH range.

Extreme pH can alter the structure and chemical properties of an enzyme and reduce its activity.

Remember: Different enzymes can have different optimum pH values.

10. Effect of Substrate Concentration

Increasing substrate concentration generally increases the reaction rate when enzyme active sites are available.

As substrate concentration becomes very high, most available active sites become occupied.

The reaction rate then approaches a maximum under those conditions.

More Substrate → More Enzyme-Substrate Complexes → Higher Rate

Until Enzyme Saturation

11. Enzyme Saturation

Enzyme saturation occurs when most or all available active sites are occupied by substrate molecules.

At saturation, adding more substrate does not produce a proportional increase in reaction rate because the available enzyme molecules are already working at or near their maximum rate.

MDCAT Point: Saturation depends on the availability of enzyme active sites.

12. Enzyme Inhibition

An inhibitor is a substance that decreases the activity of an enzyme.

Competitive Inhibition

A competitive inhibitor competes with the substrate for the enzyme's active site.

  • It binds at the active site.
  • It competes with the substrate.
  • It reduces substrate binding.
  • Increasing substrate concentration can reduce its effect in many cases.

Non-Competitive Inhibition

A non-competitive inhibitor binds at a site other than the active site and can change enzyme shape or activity.

Competitive Non-Competitive
Competes for active site Binds at another site
Competes with substrate Can alter enzyme shape and activity

13. Cofactors and Coenzymes

Cofactors

Some enzymes require non-protein components called cofactors for proper activity.

Cofactors can include inorganic ions such as metal ions.

Coenzymes

Coenzymes are organic molecules that assist enzymes during biochemical reactions. Many coenzymes are derived from vitamins.

Remember: Cofactor = non-protein helper.
Coenzyme = organic type of enzyme helper.

14. Apoenzyme and Holoenzyme

Apoenzyme

The protein component of an enzyme that requires a non-protein component is called the apoenzyme.

Holoenzyme

The complete active enzyme consisting of the apoenzyme together with its required cofactor is called a holoenzyme.

Apoenzyme + Cofactor = Holoenzyme

15. Denaturation of Enzymes

Denaturation is the disruption of the normal three-dimensional structure of a protein.

Since enzyme activity depends on its structure, denaturation can cause loss of enzyme activity.

  • High temperature can cause denaturation.
  • Extreme pH can cause denaturation.
  • The active site may lose its proper shape.

16. Intracellular and Extracellular Enzymes

Intracellular Enzymes

Intracellular enzymes function inside cells.

Extracellular Enzymes

Extracellular enzymes are released outside cells and function in the surrounding environment.

Example: Digestive enzymes released into the digestive tract are examples of extracellular enzymes.

17. Important Digestive Enzymes

Enzyme Major Action
Amylase Acts on starch
Pepsin Digests proteins in the stomach
Trypsin Digests proteins in the small intestine
Lipase Digests lipids
Sucrase Breaks down sucrose
Lactase Breaks down lactose

📝 MDCAT Practice MCQs

1. Enzymes are biological:

A. Hormones
B. Catalysts
C. Vitamins
D. Lipids

Answer: B. Catalysts

2. Enzymes speed up reactions mainly by:

A. Increasing activation energy
B. Decreasing activation energy
C. Increasing product concentration
D. Changing the final products

Answer: B. Decreasing activation energy

3. The region of an enzyme where the substrate binds is called:

A. Nucleus
B. Active site
C. Cell wall
D. Cytoplasm

Answer: B. Active site

4. The molecule upon which an enzyme acts is called:

A. Product
B. Cofactor
C. Substrate
D. Coenzyme

Answer: C. Substrate

5. Which model suggests that the active site adjusts its shape when the substrate binds?

A. Lock-and-key model
B. Induced-fit model
C. Fluid mosaic model
D. Cell theory

Answer: B. Induced-fit model

6. Excessive heat can reduce enzyme activity by causing:

A. Replication
B. Denaturation
C. Translation
D. Diffusion

Answer: B. Denaturation

7. A competitive inhibitor competes with the substrate for the:

A. Nucleus
B. Active site
C. Cell wall
D. Ribosome

Answer: B. Active site

8. Which enzyme acts on starch?

A. Lipase
B. Amylase
C. Lactase
D. Pepsin

Answer: B. Amylase

9. The protein component of a complex enzyme is called:

A. Apoenzyme
B. Coenzyme
C. Substrate
D. Product

Answer: A. Apoenzyme

10. Apoenzyme combined with its required cofactor forms:

A. Substrate
B. Holoenzyme
C. Product
D. Inhibitor

Answer: B. Holoenzyme

11. Which factor can strongly affect enzyme activity?

A. Temperature
B. pH
C. Substrate concentration
D. All of the above

Answer: D. All of the above

12. What happens when an enzyme becomes saturated with substrate?

A. All enzymes disappear
B. Most active sites are occupied
C. Activation energy becomes infinite
D. Substrate stops existing

Answer: B. Most active sites are occupied
