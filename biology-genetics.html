1. What is Genetics?

Genetics is the branch of biology that deals with heredity and variation in organisms.

Heredity is the transmission of characteristics from parents to offspring.

Variation refers to differences in characteristics among individuals of the same species.

MDCAT Point: Genetics helps us understand how traits are inherited from parents and why offspring are not always identical to their parents.

2. DNA, Genes and Chromosomes

DNA

DNA stands for Deoxyribonucleic Acid. It contains genetic information in most organisms.

Gene

A gene is a segment of DNA that contains information for a particular functional product or trait.

Chromosome

A chromosome is a DNA molecule associated with proteins. Chromosomes carry many genes.

Genome

The genome is the complete genetic material of an organism.

Remember: DNA → contains genes → genes are located on chromosomes.

3. Important Genetic Terms

Term Meaning
Allele An alternative form of a gene.
Genotype The genetic makeup of an organism.
Phenotype The observable characteristics of an organism.
Homozygous Having two identical alleles, such as TT or tt.
Heterozygous Having two different alleles, such as Tt.
Dominant allele An allele that can express its effect in a heterozygous condition.
Recessive allele An allele whose effect may be masked by a dominant allele.

4. Gregor Mendel

Gregor Mendel is known as the Father of Genetics.

Mendel studied inheritance using pea plants (Pisum sativum).

He selected pea plants because they have several clear contrasting traits and can undergo self-pollination and cross-pollination.

MDCAT Tip: Remember: Gregor Mendel = Father of Genetics = Pea plant experiments.

5. Mendel's Laws

Law of Segregation

The two alleles of a gene separate from each other during gamete formation, so each gamete receives only one allele.

Simple idea: One pair of alleles separates during gamete formation.

Law of Independent Assortment

During gamete formation, alleles of different genes can assort independently of one another, provided the genes are not linked in a way that prevents independent assortment.

6. Monohybrid Cross

A monohybrid cross studies the inheritance of one characteristic.

Suppose T represents tallness and t represents shortness.

Cross:

Tt × Tt

T t
T TT Tt
t Tt tt

Genotypic ratio: 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt

If T is completely dominant, the phenotypic ratio is: 3 tall : 1 short

Important: The classic monohybrid phenotypic ratio is 3:1.

7. Dihybrid Cross

A dihybrid cross studies the inheritance of two characteristics at the same time.

Under suitable conditions, a classic dihybrid cross can produce a phenotypic ratio of:

9 : 3 : 3 : 1

This ratio is associated with Mendel's law of independent assortment when the relevant genes assort independently.

8. Punnett Square

A Punnett square is a diagram used to predict possible combinations of alleles in offspring.

It helps determine expected genotypes and phenotypes from a genetic cross.

Remember: A Punnett square predicts probabilities. It does not guarantee the exact outcome in every family or group of offspring.

9. Incomplete Dominance

In incomplete dominance, neither allele completely masks the other.

The heterozygous offspring shows an intermediate phenotype.

A classic example is flower colour in some varieties of snapdragons, where red × white may produce pink offspring.

MDCAT Point: Incomplete dominance often gives a phenotypic ratio of 1 : 2 : 1 in the F2 generation.

10. Codominance

In codominance, both alleles are expressed in the heterozygous condition.

A famous example is the AB blood group, where both A and B alleles are expressed.

Easy difference:
Incomplete dominance → intermediate phenotype.
Codominance → both alleles are expressed.

11. ABO Blood Group System

The ABO blood group system is controlled by three alleles:

  • IA
  • IB
  • i
Blood Group Possible Genotypes
A IAIA or IAi
B IBIB or IBi
AB IAIB
O ii
Important: IA and IB are codominant, while i is recessive to both.

12. Sex Determination

In humans, females normally have XX sex chromosomes and males normally have XY.

The egg normally carries an X chromosome, while sperm can carry either X or Y.

Egg Sperm Possible Child
X X XX
X Y XY
MDCAT Point: The sperm contributes either X or Y chromosome in the usual human XX/XY system.

13. Sex-Linked Inheritance

Some genes are located on sex chromosomes, especially the X chromosome. Traits controlled by such genes are called sex-linked traits.

Examples commonly studied include red-green colour blindness and haemophilia.

Key idea: X-linked recessive conditions are often more common in males because males normally have only one X chromosome.

14. Mutation

A mutation is a change in genetic material.

Mutations can occur naturally or can be caused by certain environmental agents called mutagens.

Examples of Mutagens

  • Some forms of radiation
  • Certain chemicals
  • Some biological agents

Mutations can be harmful, neutral, or sometimes beneficial, depending on the mutation and environment.

15. DNA Replication

DNA replication is the process by which DNA makes a copy of itself.

DNA replication is described as semiconservative because each daughter DNA molecule contains one original strand and one newly synthesized strand.

MDCAT Point: DNA replication occurs before cell division so that genetic information can be passed to daughter cells.

16. Transcription

Transcription is the process in which information from DNA is used to produce an RNA molecule.

In eukaryotic cells, transcription mainly occurs in the nucleus.

DNA → RNA = Transcription

17. Translation

Translation is the process in which information carried by mRNA is used to synthesize a polypeptide.

Translation occurs at ribosomes.

RNA → Protein = Translation

20. MDCAT Exam Tip

Genetics questions often test definitions, Mendelian ratios, genotype vs phenotype, dominance relationships, blood groups, sex-linked inheritance and DNA → RNA → protein.

Most important ratios to remember: 3:1 and 1:2:1 for monohybrid situations, and 9:3:3:1 for a classic dihybrid cross under independent assortment.
