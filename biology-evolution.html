Evolution

Evolution is the change in inherited characteristics of populations over generations. These changes can result in the formation of adaptations and, over long periods, contribute to the diversity of life.

MDCAT Point: Evolution occurs in populations over generations. Individuals do not evolve simply because they need a new characteristic.

1. Variation

Members of the same species are not exactly identical. Differences between individuals are called variation.

Sources of variation

  • Mutation
  • Meiosis and crossing over
  • Independent assortment of chromosomes
  • Random fertilization
  • Gene flow between populations
Variation provides the raw material on which natural selection can act.

2. Mutation

A mutation is a change in the DNA sequence. Mutations can create new alleles and may be harmful, beneficial, or have little effect.

Important points

  • Mutations can introduce new genetic variation.
  • Some mutations are neutral.
  • Some mutations can be harmful.
  • Some mutations can provide an advantage in a particular environment.

3. Natural Selection

Natural selection is the process by which individuals with heritable characteristics that improve survival or reproduction tend to leave more offspring.

Basic idea

  1. Variation exists in a population.
  2. Some variations are heritable.
  3. Organisms produce more offspring than can survive.
  4. There is competition for limited resources.
  5. Individuals with advantageous inherited traits may survive and reproduce more successfully.
  6. Those traits can become more common in the population over generations.
Remember: Natural selection acts on existing heritable variation. The environment does not directly create a useful trait because an organism needs it.

4. Adaptation

An adaptation is an inherited characteristic that improves an organism's ability to survive and reproduce in a particular environment.

Examples

  • Camouflage in prey animals
  • Thick fur in animals living in cold environments
  • Streamlined bodies in aquatic animals
  • Specialized beaks in birds
  • Water-conserving features in desert plants

5. Artificial Selection

Artificial selection occurs when humans choose organisms with desirable characteristics and breed them to produce offspring with selected traits.

Examples

  • Selective breeding of crops
  • Breeding livestock for increased milk production
  • Breeding dogs for particular characteristics
Natural selection → environment determines which heritable traits provide an advantage.

Artificial selection → humans choose which organisms reproduce.

6. Evidence for Evolution

Scientists use several lines of evidence to study evolutionary relationships.

Fossils

Fossils provide evidence about organisms that lived in the past. Fossil sequences can show changes in groups of organisms through geological time.

Comparative Anatomy

Comparing body structures can reveal evolutionary relationships.

7. Homologous Structures

Homologous structures have similar underlying anatomy because of common ancestry, although they may perform different functions.

Example: the forelimbs of humans, bats and whales have similar basic skeletal patterns but are adapted for different functions.

Homologous structures are evidence of common ancestry.

8. Analogous Structures

Analogous structures perform similar functions but have different underlying structural origins.

Example: wings of birds and wings of insects both help in flight, but their structures and evolutionary origins are different.

Homologous: similar basic structure, possibly different functions.

Analogous: similar function, different basic structure and evolutionary origin.

9. Vestigial Structures

A vestigial structure is a reduced structure that has little or no significant function in its present form but is inherited from ancestors in which it had a greater function.

Examples often discussed include the human coccyx and the reduced pelvic bones of some snakes and whales.

10. Embryological Evidence

Similarities in early developmental stages among different organisms can provide evidence of evolutionary relationships.

Developmental patterns, however, must be interpreted carefully rather than assuming that embryos of different species are identical.

11. Molecular Evidence

DNA and protein sequences can be compared between organisms. Greater molecular similarity generally indicates a more recent common ancestry.

Important examples

  • DNA sequence comparison
  • Protein sequence comparison
  • Comparison of genes and genomes

12. Speciation

Speciation is the formation of new species. It can occur when populations become genetically different and eventually become reproductively isolated.

Factors that can contribute

  • Geographical isolation
  • Mutation
  • Natural selection
  • Genetic drift
  • Reduced gene flow

13. Geographical Isolation

When a population becomes separated by a physical barrier such as a mountain, river or large geographical distance, gene flow between the populations may decrease.

Over many generations, the separated populations may accumulate genetic differences.

14. Genetic Drift

Genetic drift is a random change in allele frequencies, especially important in small populations.

Two important examples

Founder effect: A new population is established by a small number of individuals from a larger population.

Bottleneck effect: A population is greatly reduced in size by an event, leaving a smaller population whose allele frequencies may differ from the original population.

15. Darwin and Natural Selection

Charles Darwin helped develop the theory of evolution by natural selection. He proposed that organisms with advantageous heritable characteristics tend to survive and reproduce more successfully.

Darwin's theory explains how natural selection can produce changes in populations over generations.

Quick Revision ⭐

  • Evolution = change in inherited characteristics of populations over generations.
  • Variation is necessary for natural selection.
  • Mutation can create new alleles.
  • Natural selection acts on heritable variation.
  • Adaptations improve survival or reproduction in particular environments.
  • Homologous structures support common ancestry.
  • Analogous structures have similar functions but different structural origins.
  • Fossils provide evidence of past life.
  • DNA and protein comparisons provide molecular evidence.
  • Speciation is the formation of new species.
  • Genetic drift is a random change in allele frequencies.
  • Founder effect and bottleneck effect are examples of genetic drift.

MDCAT Practice MCQs

1. Evolution is best described as:

A) Change in an individual during its lifetime
B) Change in inherited characteristics of populations over generations
C) Growth of an organism
D) Movement of organisms

Answer: B) Change in inherited characteristics of populations over generations

2. Which can introduce new alleles into a population?

A) Mutation
B) Digestion
C) Respiration
D) Excretion

Answer: A) Mutation

3. Natural selection acts directly on:

A) Heritable phenotypic variation
B) Rocks
C) Fossils only
D) Individual DNA molecules only

Answer: A) Heritable phenotypic variation

4. Which structure is an example of homologous structures?

A) Bird wing and insect wing
B) Human arm and whale flipper
C) Human eye and camera
D) Fish fin and car tyre

Answer: B) Human arm and whale flipper

5. Analogous structures have:

A) Similar function but different structural origins
B) Identical structure and function
C) No function
D) Identical DNA sequences

Answer: A) Similar function but different structural origins

6. Fossils provide evidence about:

A) Only living organisms
B) Past organisms and life forms
C) Future species
D) Only bacteria

Answer: B) Past organisms and life forms

7. Greater DNA similarity between two organisms generally suggests:

A) More recent common ancestry
B) No relationship
C) Different kingdoms
D) No evolution

Answer: A) More recent common ancestry

8. Formation of a new species is called:

A) Mutation
B) Speciation
C) Translation
D) Respiration

Answer: B) Speciation

9. Genetic drift is particularly important in:

A) Very large populations only
B) Small populations
C) Non-living matter
D) Water molecules

Answer: B) Small populations

10. The founder effect is associated with:

A) A new population started by a small number of individuals
B) Unlimited gene flow
C) Digestion
D) Photosynthesis

Answer: A) A new population started by a small number of individuals

11. Artificial selection is controlled mainly by:

A) Humans
B) Weather only
C) Bacteria
D) Fossils

Answer: A) Humans

12. Which provides raw material for natural selection?

A) Heritable variation
B) Water only
C) Oxygen only
D) Body temperature

Answer: A) Heritable variation

13. A physical barrier separating populations can reduce:

A) Gene flow
B) Mutation completely
C) DNA formation completely
D) Respiration

Answer: A) Gene flow

14. Which scientist is strongly associated with natural selection?

A) Charles Darwin
B) Louis Pasteur
C) Robert Hooke
D) Gregor Mendel

Answer: A) Charles Darwin

15. A bottleneck effect occurs when:

A) A population is greatly reduced in size
B) A population doubles immediately
C) All mutations stop
D) All organisms become identical

Answer: A) A population is greatly reduced in size

Exam Tips 🎯

  • Learn the difference between homologous and analogous structures.
  • Remember that natural selection acts on heritable variation.
  • Mutation can introduce new alleles.
  • Evolution is measured at the population level across generations.
  • Know founder effect and bottleneck effect.
  • DNA and protein similarities can provide evidence of common ancestry.
  • Speciation means formation of new species.
