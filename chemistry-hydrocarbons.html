Unit 14: Chemistry of Hydrocarbons

Definition: Hydrocarbons are organic compounds that contain only carbon and hydrogen.
MDCAT Focus: This unit covers alkanes, alkenes, benzene, alkynes, nomenclature, preparation, reactions and the difference between substitution and addition reactions.

1. Classification of Hydrocarbons

Type Main Feature Example
Alkanes Only C–C single bonds CH₄
Alkenes At least one C=C double bond C₂H₄
Alkynes At least one C≡C triple bond C₂H₂
Aromatic hydrocarbons Aromatic ring system Benzene, C₆H₆

2. Alkanes

Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons containing only single carbon-carbon bonds.
General formula of open-chain alkanes: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂
Alkane Formula
Methane CH₄
Ethane C₂H₆
Propane C₃H₈
Butane C₄H₁₀
Pentane C₅H₁₂

3. Alkane Nomenclature

In systematic naming, the longest continuous carbon chain is selected as the parent chain.

  1. Find the longest carbon chain.
  2. Number the chain from the end giving the substituent the lowest possible number.
  3. Identify substituents.
  4. Use the appropriate prefix and parent name.
Example:

CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃

Four carbon atoms → Butane

4. Alkyl Groups

Alkyl groups are formed by removing one hydrogen atom from an alkane.

Alkane Alkyl Group
Methane, CH₄ Methyl, –CH₃
Ethane, C₂H₆ Ethyl, –C₂H₅
Propane, C₃H₈ Propyl, –C₃H₇
Butane, C₄H₁₀ Butyl, –C₄H₉

5. Free-Radical Substitution

Alkanes commonly undergo substitution reactions with halogens under suitable conditions such as light.
General reaction:

RH + X₂ → RX + HX

The reaction proceeds through free-radical steps.

Initiation

Cl₂ → 2Cl•
in the presence of suitable light

Propagation

CH₄ + Cl• → CH₃• + HCl

CH₃• + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl•

Termination

Free radicals combine with one another, ending the chain reaction.

Remember the three main stages: Initiation → Propagation → Termination

6. Alkenes

Alkenes are unsaturated hydrocarbons containing at least one carbon-carbon double bond.
General formula of simple open-chain alkenes with one double bond: CₙH₂ₙ
Examples:

Ethene → C₂H₄
Propene → C₃H₆
Butene → C₄H₈

7. Structure of the C=C Bond

A carbon-carbon double bond consists of:

  • One sigma (σ) bond
  • One pi (π) bond
The double bond restricts rotation around the C=C bond.

This restricted rotation is important for geometrical stereoisomerism.

8. Alkene Nomenclature

  1. Select the longest chain containing the double bond.
  2. Number the chain so the double bond receives the lowest possible number.
  3. Replace the "-ane" ending with "-ene".
  4. Give the position of the double bond when required.
Example:

CH₂=CH–CH₃

Three carbon atoms with a double bond → Propene

9. Preparation of Alkenes

One important method of preparing alkenes is elimination from suitable saturated compounds.

Alcohol → Alkene + H₂O

This is called dehydration.
Ethanol can undergo dehydration to form ethene:

C₂H₅OH → C₂H₄ + H₂O

10. Reactivity of Alkenes

Alkenes are generally more reactive than alkanes because the electron-rich π bond can participate in addition reactions.

The characteristic reaction of alkenes is addition.

11. Addition Reactions of Alkenes

In an addition reaction, atoms or groups are added across the carbon-carbon double bond.

C=C + A–B → C–C
with A and B added across the double bond

Hydrogenation

Alkene + H₂ → Alkane

Halogen Addition

Alkene + Br₂ → Dibromoalkane

Hydrogen Halide Addition

Alkene + HCl → Chloroalkane

12. Benzene

Benzene is an important aromatic hydrocarbon with molecular formula C₆H₆.

Benzene contains six carbon atoms arranged in a planar ring. Its π electrons are delocalized over the ring.

Molecular formula: C₆H₆

13. Benzene Molecular Orbital Concept

Each carbon atom in benzene contributes a p orbital to the delocalized π system.

The p orbitals overlap around the ring, allowing the π electrons to become delocalized.

Benzene is commonly represented by resonance structures, but its actual structure has delocalized π electrons.

14. Resonance in Benzene

Resonance is used to represent a molecule whose electron distribution cannot be described adequately by a single conventional Lewis structure.

Benzene is often represented using alternating double bonds or a circle inside the ring to indicate electron delocalization.

The C–C bonds in benzene are equivalent because of π-electron delocalization.

15. Electrophilic Substitution in Benzene

Benzene commonly undergoes electrophilic aromatic substitution reactions.

In these reactions, an electrophile replaces a hydrogen atom on the aromatic ring while aromaticity is ultimately restored.

Example:

Benzene + HNO₃ → Nitrobenzene

under suitable nitration conditions.
Unlike many alkenes, benzene tends to undergo substitution rather than simple addition because maintaining aromatic stabilization is important.

16. Alkynes

Alkynes are unsaturated hydrocarbons containing at least one carbon-carbon triple bond.
General formula of simple open-chain alkynes with one triple bond: CₙH₂ₙ₋₂
Examples:

Ethyne → C₂H₂
Propyne → C₃H₄
Butyne → C₄H₆

17. Structure of the C≡C Bond

A carbon-carbon triple bond consists of:

  • One sigma (σ) bond
  • Two pi (π) bonds
Triple bond: 1σ + 2π

The carbon atoms involved in a simple alkyne have linear arrangement around the triple bond.

18. Alkyne Nomenclature

  1. Select the longest chain containing the triple bond.
  2. Number the chain so the triple bond gets the lowest possible number.
  3. Replace the "-ane" ending with "-yne".
  4. State the position of the triple bond when needed.
Example:

CH≡C–CH₃

Three carbon atoms with a triple bond → Prop-1-yne or commonly propyne.

19. Preparation of Alkynes

Alkynes can be prepared through elimination reactions that remove hydrogen halides from suitable dihalides.

Dihalide → Alkyne + HX-containing products

under suitable elimination conditions.

Ethyne can also be produced industrially from suitable carbon sources and has important chemical applications.

20. Acidity of Terminal Alkynes

Terminal alkynes contain a hydrogen attached directly to a triple-bonded carbon.
Terminal alkynes are more acidic than alkanes and alkenes because the conjugate base is stabilized by the sp-hybridized carbon.
Example of a terminal alkyne:

HC≡CH
Ethyne

21. Reactions of Alkynes

Alkynes can undergo addition reactions because of their π bonds.

  • Hydrogenation
  • Halogen addition
  • Hydrogen halide addition
  • Hydration under suitable conditions
Addition can occur across the triple bond, and depending on the reaction conditions the product can be an alkene or ultimately an alkane.

22. Substitution vs Addition

Feature Substitution Addition
Main idea One atom/group replaces another Atoms/groups add across a multiple bond
Common example Alkane halogenation Alkene + H₂
Commonly associated with Alkanes and aromatic compounds Alkenes and alkynes
MDCAT Tip: Alkanes commonly undergo substitution, while alkenes and alkynes commonly undergo addition.

23. Important Formulas

Alkanes: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂

Alkenes: CₙH₂ₙ

Alkynes: CₙH₂ₙ₋₂

Benzene: C₆H₆

24. Quick Revision

  • Hydrocarbons contain only carbon and hydrogen.
  • Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons.
  • Alkanes have the general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂.
  • Alkanes commonly undergo free-radical substitution.
  • Free-radical substitution has initiation, propagation and termination steps.
  • Alkenes contain C=C double bonds.
  • Alkenes have the general formula CₙH₂ₙ for simple open-chain compounds with one double bond.
  • A C=C bond contains one σ and one π bond.
  • Alkenes commonly undergo addition reactions.
  • Benzene has formula C₆H₆.
  • Benzene has delocalized π electrons.
  • Benzene commonly undergoes electrophilic aromatic substitution.
  • Alkynes contain C≡C triple bonds.
  • Alkynes have the general formula CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ for simple open-chain compounds with one triple bond.
  • A C≡C bond contains one σ and two π bonds.
  • Terminal alkynes have an acidic hydrogen.
  • Alkynes commonly undergo addition reactions.
  • Alkanes → substitution; alkenes/alkynes → addition is a useful MDCAT rule.

25. MDCAT Practice MCQs

1. Hydrocarbons contain:

A. Carbon and oxygen
B. Carbon and hydrogen
C. Hydrogen and nitrogen
D. Oxygen and sulfur

Answer: B. Carbon and hydrogen

2. The general formula of open-chain alkanes is:

A. CₙH₂ₙ
B. CₙH₂ₙ₋₂
C. CₙH₂ₙ₊₂
D. CₙHₙ

Answer: C. CₙH₂ₙ₊₂

3. Which hydrocarbon contains a double bond?

A. Alkane
B. Alkene
C. Alkyl group
D. Methane

Answer: B. Alkene

4. The general formula of simple open-chain alkenes with one double bond is:

A. CₙH₂ₙ₊₂
B. CₙH₂ₙ
C. CₙH₂ₙ₋₂
D. CₙHₙ

Answer: B. CₙH₂ₙ

5. A carbon-carbon double bond contains:

A. 2 sigma bonds
B. 2 pi bonds
C. 1 sigma and 1 pi bond
D. 1 sigma only

Answer: C. 1 sigma and 1 pi bond

6. The characteristic reaction of alkenes is commonly:

A. Addition
B. Substitution only
C. Neutralization
D. Precipitation

Answer: A. Addition

7. The molecular formula of benzene is:

A. C₆H₁₂
B. C₆H₆
C. C₅H₆
D. C₇H₈

Answer: B. C₆H₆

8. Benzene contains:

A. Localized π electrons only
B. Delocalized π electrons
C. No π electrons
D. Only ionic bonds

Answer: B. Delocalized π electrons

9. Benzene commonly undergoes:

A. Electrophilic aromatic substitution
B. Free-radical polymerization only
C. Neutralization
D. Hydrolysis only

Answer: A. Electrophilic aromatic substitution

10. The general formula of simple open-chain alkynes with one triple bond is:

A. CₙH₂ₙ₊₂
B. CₙH₂ₙ
C. CₙH₂ₙ₋₂
D. CₙH₂ₙ₊₁

Answer: C. CₙH₂ₙ₋₂

11. A carbon-carbon triple bond contains:

A. 3 sigma bonds
B. 2 sigma and 1 pi bond
C. 1 sigma and 2 pi bonds
D. 3 pi bonds

Answer: C. 1 sigma and 2 pi bonds

12. Which compound is a terminal alkyne?

A. CH₃–CH₃
B. CH₂=CH₂
C. HC≡CH
D. C₆H₆

Answer: C. HC≡CH

13. Free-radical substitution of alkanes commonly involves:

A. Initiation, propagation and termination
B. Only addition
C. Only oxidation
D. Only hydrolysis

Answer: A. Initiation, propagation and termination

14. Which reaction adds atoms across a double bond?

A. Substitution
B. Addition
C. Condensation
D. Neutralization

Answer: B. Addition

15. Which statement is correct?

A. Alkanes commonly undergo addition
B. Alkenes contain no π bond
C. Alkynes contain a triple bond
D. Benzene contains no carbon

Answer: C. Alkynes contain a triple bond

26. MDCAT Exam Tips

  • Memorize the formulas: alkane CₙH₂ₙ₊₂, alkene CₙH₂ₙ and alkyne CₙH₂ₙ₋₂.
  • Remember that C=C contains 1σ + 1π.
  • Remember that C≡C contains 1σ + 2π.
  • Know the three stages of free-radical substitution.
  • Learn basic alkane, alkene and alkyne nomenclature.
  • Remember benzene's formula: C₆H₆.
  • Understand benzene's delocalized π-electron system.
  • Remember that benzene commonly undergoes electrophilic substitution.
  • Know why alkenes and alkynes readily undergo addition.
  • Remember that terminal alkynes have an acidic hydrogen.
  • Practice identifying whether a reaction is substitution or addition.
